# What Roam Is

> *"You told yourself you'd go outside today. It's 5:30. You didn't."*

---

## The Problem It's Solving

Roam is built around a specific, quiet kind of suffering that doesn't have a dramatic name: the flatness of a life lived mostly indoors.

Not depression. Not crisis. Just — flat. You have a job, a to-do list, screens everywhere, and somewhere along the way the door stopped opening unless you had an errand on the other side of it. 73% of adults feel lonely regularly. 1 in 5 Americans move cities every five years, and it takes four months on average to form a real social circle in a new place. And yet the apps that tried to fix this — Strava, Peloton, ClassPass — all attack the wrong problem. They gamify fitness. They treat the body as a project. 60% of people delete them within 30 days, not because they're lazy, but because **the problem was never fitness. The problem is that nobody's giving you a reason to put your shoes on.**

Roam's thesis: if someone just said *hey, let's go* — you'd go.

---

## What It Actually Is

Roam is a social walking app. Two things at its core:

1. **Echo** — a voice companion that walks with you when you're alone
2. **A heat map** — a fog-of-war city map that your friend group fills in together

That's it. Everything else in the product exists to make those two things feel real.

---

## Echo

Echo is the emotional center of the app. It's not a wellness assistant. It's not a coach. It's not a chatbot.

Echo is described internally as: *"a warm, curious voice that walks with you. Like a friend in your pocket."*

When you open the app and tap go, Echo speaks first:

> *"Hey. Glad you're out here. What are we walking toward today, or are we just walking?"*

That line is doing a lot of work. It's not "you've got this!" It's not a motivational quote. It's what a real friend says when they're glad to see you without making it a thing. The tone is deliberate — warm but low-energy, present without being performative.

Echo's rules, by design:

- **Never** mentions exercise, fitness, steps, calories, weight, or health benefits. If you bring it up, Echo gently changes the subject.
- **Never** says "good for you" or "great job." That would make walking feel like a reward for completing a chore.
- **Never** asks "how are you feeling?" — that's therapy language. Echo asks about concrete things: what you ate, what you're listening to, what made you turn down that street.
- **Never** gives unsolicited advice. Gives it once if asked, then stops.
- **Never** mentions being an AI.

Echo asks one question at a time, then lets silence happen. Sentences are 5–15 words — made for being heard through a phone speaker on a sidewalk. Occasionally dry, sometimes just an "Mm." It notices things — the light, a turn you don't usually take, a street you've never walked before.

> *"The light's nice right now. You notice that?"*

> *"Oh — new street for us. What made you turn here?"*

> *"What's the best thing you ate today?"*

The closer, when you end a walk:

> *"Good walk. Same time tomorrow?"*

That's the whole pitch. Not a tracker. Not a coach. A friend.

## The Heat Map

As you walk, the city reveals itself. Streets you've never walked clear from fog. Streets you walk again deepen in color — cool greens warming to amber and gold as you return. Over 30 days, the routes that matter to you radiate outward from your home like veins in a leaf.

This isn't a step counter with a map skin. It's a **portrait of your life outside.** Every tile on the map is roughly a city block, and each one has memory — it knows how many times you've been there, and it shows you.

At the end of a walk you see: your path as a glowing line, the tiles you uncovered, the fog still covering the rest of the city. The map is a record of everywhere you've been, and an invitation to everywhere you haven't.

## Group Mode

For people like Kobe — new to a city, wanting to explore but not wanting to feel like a tourist — Roam has a group mode.

You scan a code, join your friends' group, and now everyone's walking independently but their tiles combine on one shared map. The heat map deepens where multiple people walk the same area. Hotspots glow where everyone congregates.

There's a leaderboard: who discovered the most new tiles, who found a street nobody else has walked yet. It's not competitive in a toxic way — it's the kind of friendly score-keeping that makes you want to go one block further.

> *Kobe: 18 tiles. Priya: 15. Marcus: 12.*

Priya walked Broadway the day before Kobe. When Kobe opens the app, her tiles overlap his at Cal Anderson Park — the park glows a little warmer. She found a coffee shop he missed. The city isn't generic anymore. It's theirs, built tile by tile, a constellation of shared memory.

**Group streaks** work socially: as long as one person in the group walks today, the streak stays alive. Accountability without guilt.

## The Two People Roam Is For

### Jordan — the one stuck inside

Jordan is 29. Knowledge worker. Hybrid, mostly remote. Lives with a partner but spends most of the day alone in front of a laptop. Hasn't seen friends in person in three weeks. Tried Strava, Peloton, ClassPass — bounced off all of them. The dominant feeling is flatness. Not sadness. Just flat.

Jordan's Tuesday looks like: wake up, camera-off standup, delivery lunch at the desk, 4:45 PM and the flat feeling hits. Then at 5:30, the app. Echo says hey. Jordan puts shoes on.

At 5:52, a street Jordan has never walked. Echo: *"Oh — new street for us. What made you turn here?"* Jordan laughs for the first time that day. By 6:15, 12 new tiles on the map, streak at 1, and Echo's goodbye. At 10:30 PM, Jordan opens the app one more time just to look at the small glowing line. Tomorrow's already there.

**Two minutes of reason. One tap to say yes. That's the whole thing.**

### Kobe — the one new to the city

Kobe is 24. Just moved to Seattle for his first job. Knows three people. Wants to explore the city but doesn't want to feel like a tourist. Has coworkers who'd explore with him if someone just picked the route.

Monday: empty apartment, suitcase and a laptop. Coworker Priya sends him a group code. He scans it — three names appear on a shared map. Zero tiles.

Tuesday: first walk with Echo through Capitol Hill, Volunteer Park to Cal Anderson. 18 tiles. Wednesday: Luna (the dog) gets two extra tiles if they go one block further, and an errand reminder pops up — milk at Fred Meyer, 3 blocks east. Thursday: Marcus fills in Pike/Pine, the triple-overlap corridor glows on the leaderboard.

By Friday: 52 tiles between three people in one week. 2 notes saved, 1 errand done, 1 dog walked. The map looks like a constellation. **Kobe knows Capitol Hill now — not like a tourist.**

## The Onboarding

Three screens. No account.

1. *"Your city is hidden in fog. Every walk you take reveals a little more. Over time, your map becomes a record of everywhere you've been."*
2. *"Meet Echo. Your walking companion. Echo walks with you, chats, and notices things along the way. Like a friend in your pocket."*
3. *"One thing before we go. Echo needs to see where you are so you can walk together. Your location stays on your device."* — Allow location / Skip for now.

Both choices proceed. You're walking in under 60 seconds.

## The Demo

The website has an interactive demo that runs entirely in-browser — no download, no account. Two personas:

**Jordan's walk** — a solo route through UW Seattle campus. Echo greets you, asks what you're walking off, notices a new street, asks what you ate. The fog lifts behind you. Walk ends: *"Good walk. Same time tomorrow?"*

**Kobe's walk** — a group demo through Capitol Hill. Kobe, Priya, and Marcus each walk their own route in sequence. You watch the tiles accumulate, the overlaps deepen, the leaderboard shift. Echo narrates each member's walk. By the end the map is glowing.

The demo is the pitch. Everything in it is real — the voice, the map, the tile math, the heat colors.

---

## The Emotional Promise

Roam isn't selling health. It's not selling productivity. It's not selling a better you.

It's selling the feeling of walking out the door when you didn't think you would, and having someone there when you do.

> *"Your city is waiting. Put your shoes on."*

The map remembers everywhere you've been. Echo remembers what you said. Tomorrow you'll open the app and your glowing line will be there, and the city will be a little more yours than it was yesterday.

That's the whole thing.
